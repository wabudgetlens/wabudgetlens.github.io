Washington Fiscal Performance Review

A first-pass attempt to answer a basic question: when Washington spending rose, how much was explained by population, inflation, caseload, rates, legal obligations, financing changes, and policy—and what still needs a better explanation?

Download full PDFRead methodology

Bottom line

Washington's operating budget grew materially faster than a simple population-and-general-inflation benchmark over the comparison period. That is a reason to investigate cost drivers and outcomes. It is not, by itself, evidence that the difference is waste.

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: the state has meaningful tax-design, recurring-cost, and performance-management questions, while several large spending increases can already be tied to policy, caseload, care costs, legal obligations, and financing changes.

K–12: one major driver is visible

The comparison leaves about $6.055 billion of additional state K–12 spending after the simple population-and-CPI adjustment. Using the FY2019 implementation level of the 2017–18 school-funding changes as a two-year scale check produces roughly $3.902 billion—about 64% of that amount.

The report does not treat that as a 2025–27 appropriation estimate. It uses it only to show that McCleary-era policy explains a large share of the state increase. The 2017 levy swap also means some state dollars replaced local levy support, so state and local K–12 funding should be examined together before drawing conclusions about total cost growth.

Tax structure

The report's concern is less “Washington collects uniquely high taxes” than “Washington relies heavily on particular kinds of taxes.” Sales taxes and the B&O gross-receipts tax create distributional and economic-design questions even when aggregate tax measures are not unusually high.

The report is dated October 4, 2026. Its current-law discussion includes Initiative 645 as a November 3, 2026 ballot question. That section should be refreshed after the election.

Efficiency review

The analysis intentionally does not apply a savings percentage to the full operating budget. After removing selected debt, core K–12 formula spending, and major entitlement/caseload lines, it identifies a $31.729 billion upper-bound review base. That base still contains significant constraints.

The practical implication: modest verified efficiencies are plausible. As savings targets get larger, the choices increasingly become policy decisions about eligibility, rates, workforce, program scope, or service levels rather than simple administrative cleanup.
